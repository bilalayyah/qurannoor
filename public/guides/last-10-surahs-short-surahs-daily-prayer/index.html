The Last 10 Surahs: Short Surahs for Daily Prayer

For millions of Muslims worldwide, the final ten chapters of the Noble Quran—Surahs 105 through 114 at the very end of Juz 30—are the earliest verses learned in childhood and the surahs most frequently recited inside the five daily prayers.

Despite their brevity (ranging from just three to seven ayahs each), these ten surahs encompass profound lessons on gratitude, sincerity, social kindness, pure monotheism, and seeking Allah’s protection.

Surahs 105–107: Gratitude, Security, and Everyday Kindness

Surah Al-Fil (105 · 5 ayahs) recalls how Allah protected the Sacred House in Makkah from Abraha’s army of the elephant, proving that earthly power cannot overcome divine protection. Immediately linked in meaning is Surah Quraysh (106 · 4 ayahs), reminding Makkah’s inhabitants to worship the Lord of that House out of gratitude for peaceful seasonal trade journeys, safety from fear, and daily sustenance.

Surah Al-Ma’un (107 · 7 ayahs) turns inward to social ethics and sincerity: it warns against harshly turning away orphans, failing to encourage feeding the needy, praying heedlessly to be seen by people, or withholding small everyday acts of neighborly kindness. Paired together, these opening three surahs remind worshippers that gratitude for safety must express itself in compassion toward neighbors and the vulnerable.

Surahs 108–111: Abundance, Steadfast Faith, and Humility

Surah Al-Kawthar (108 · 3 ayahs) is the shortest chapter in the Quran, comforting the Prophet with the promise of abundant goodness and urging sincere prayer and sacrifice rather than reacting to mockers. Surah Al-Kafirun (109 · 6 ayahs) draws a clear, dignified boundary in worship—affirming pure devotion without ever compromising monotheism.

Surah An-Nasr (110 · 3 ayahs) marks the opening of victory and teaches believers how to respond to success: not with arrogance, but with praise, glorification, and seeking Allah’s forgiveness. Surah Al-Masad (111 · 5 ayahs) serves as a stark reminder that wealth, lineage, and social status offer no shield when a person chooses persistent hostility against truth. Across just seventeen ayahs, these four surahs teach steadfastness under mockery, humility in victory, and moral accountability.

Surahs 112–114: Pure Monotheism and Seeking Protection

Surah Al-Ikhlas (112 · 4 ayahs) summarizes Islamic monotheism with unmatched clarity: Allah is One, Eternal and Self-Sufficient (as-Samad), neither begetting nor begotten, with none comparable unto Him in any way.

The Quran closes with two paired supplications known as al-Mu’awwidhatayn (the two chapters of seeking refuge): Surah Al-Falaq (113 · 5 ayahs), asking Allah’s protection as Lord of daybreak against outward harms, the darkness of night, and envy, and Surah An-Nas (114 · 6 ayahs), seeking refuge with the Lord, Sovereign, and God of mankind from unseen whispers within the chest. Recited morning, evening, and in daily prayers, these closing three chapters anchor the heart in pure tawhid and constant reliance on Allah.

Reciting the Last 10 Surahs With Presence in Daily Prayer

Because most worshippers recite these short surahs automatically from childhood memory, slowing down to learn the translation of each single ayah transforms daily salah. Try focusing on one Surah each week: read its Arabic carefully with Tajweed, listen to a crisp reciter such as Al-Husary or Alafasy, and read its short Tafsir notes.

Next time you recite Surah Quraysh, Al-Ma’un, or Al-Ikhlas in prayer, reflect directly on its core message rather than rushing through familiar syllables. Even three-ayah chapters carry enough spiritual depth to renew every rak’ah of your daily prayer and keep your recitation thoughtful rather than mechanical.

Teaching these ten surahs gently to children or new learners at home also builds a warm shared bond around daily prayer—turning short nightly recitations into moments of reflection on gratitude, generosity, and reliance upon Allah.

Key takeaways

  • Surahs 105–114 form the closing set of Juz 30 and are widely memorized first for daily salah.
  • Surah Al-Kawthar (108) is the shortest Surah in the Quran with 3 ayahs; Al-Ma’un (107) connects sincere prayer with caring for orphans and neighbors.
  • Surah Al-Ikhlas (112) affirms Allah’s absolute oneness in four concise verses.
  • Surahs Al-Falaq (113) and An-Nas (114) close the Quran as prayers for protection against outer harm and inner whispers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which is the shortest Surah among the last 10 Surahs?

Surah Al-Kawthar (Surah 108) is the shortest Surah in the entire Quran, consisting of 3 short ayahs (An-Nasr, Surah 110, also has 3 ayahs).

What are the Mu’awwidhatayn?

Mu’awwidhatayn means "the two chapters of seeking refuge"—referring to the final two chapters of the Quran, Surah Al-Falaq (113) and Surah An-Nas (114).

Are these 10 Surahs revealed in Makkah or Madinah?

Most of the last 10 Surahs—including Al-Fil, Quraysh, Al-Kawthar, Al-Kafirun, and Al-Ikhlas—are Makkan revelations focused on faith, gratitude, and sincerity, while An-Nasr (110) was revealed in Madinah.

How can I learn their meanings quickly?

Open Juz 30 or browse Surahs 105–114 individually on QuranNoor, listen to one short surah a day in Arabic with English or Urdu translation turned on, and focus on its main theme before praying.
